FrankScore Psychometric Trait Scoring
End-to-End Example with Five Borrowers
Traits, Behavioural Metadata, and Content (Questions and Scenarios)
1 Introduction
This report illustrates how FrankScore combines three core elements:
•Psychometric traits: a set of fifteen behavioural and attitudinal dimensions relevant for
creditworthiness.
•Behavioural metadata: forty-seven digital interaction features recorded while a borrower
completes a psychometric assessment.
•Content: questionnaire items and short scenarios that ask about real decisions, plans, and
attitudes.
For five fictional borrowers, we assume both content responses and metadata are available,
and show how these inputs can be used to derive final trait scores on a normalised scale from zero
to one. The goal is not to present final production weights, but to provide a clear, explainable
blueprint that can be calibrated with real data.
2 Conceptual Framework
FrankScore uses a three-body model:
1.Traitsare the latent psychological dimensions we want to estimate, such as Conscientious-
ness or Honesty.
2.Contentconsists of questions and mini scenarios that ask borrowers about their behaviour,
preferences, and attitudes.
3.Behavioural metadataconsists of how borrowers interact with the assessment itself: how
fast they respond, whether they skip questions, how they scroll, and so on.
For each trait:
•Content responses are converted into acontent-based trait scorein the range[0,1].
•Metadata features are transformed into abehaviour-based trait scorein the range[0,1].
•These two components are combined into a final trait value:
Traitfinal=α·Trait behaviour + (1−α)·Trait content,
whereαis a weight that can later be calibrated with real loan performance data.

5 Content Design: Question and Scenario Types
All questions and scenarios are maintained in an item bank. Each item has an identifier, a main
trait tag, an item type (such as Likert, time preference, or scenario), and a scoring rule that
maps each possible answer to a score between zero and one.
The main item types used are:
•Likert statementswith five options from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”.
•Time preference choices, such as “1,000 birr today versus 1,300 birr in one month”.
•Risk choice scenarioscomparing safe versus risky options.
•Honesty and integrity scenarios, for example, how to react when a lender sends too
much money by mistake.
•Financial decision tasksthat compare loans or repayment plans.
•Planning horizon questions, asking how far ahead the borrower usually plans.
•Spending versus saving questions, asking whether extra income is first used to buy or
to save or repay.

6 Scoring Methodology
6.1 Normalisation
All content item scores are explicitly designed to lie in the interval[0,1]. For Likert items with
five response options, positive items use
item_score=r−1
4,
whereris the chosen response from one to five. Reverse-keyed items invert the scale.
Behavioural metadata variables are normalised based on observed ranges in the data. In
addition, for each trait the direction is aligned so that higher values always correspond to more
desirable behaviour for that trait, by transformations such as
skip_good= 1−skip_rate,idle_good= 1−idle_time_ratio.
6.2 Content-Based Trait Scores
For each trait, a subset of items is tagged in the item bank as measuring that trait. For a given
borrower and trait, the content-based score is calculated as the average of the item scores for
that trait:
Traitcontent =1
nn/summationdisplay
j=1item_score j,
wherenis the number of items that map to the trait. In a more advanced implementation, item
weights can be non-equal, or item parameters can be estimated using item response theory.
6.3 Behaviour-Based Trait Scores from Metadata
Behaviour-based trait scores are derived from the forty-seven metadata variables. For each
metadata feature we define a transformed version that reflects “good” behaviour for particular
traits. For example:
completion_good=completion_status,
sessions_good= 1−sessions_count,
idle_good= 1−idle_time_ratio,
skip_good= 1−skip_rate,
hard_good=hard_items_completed_rate,
speed_impulsive= 1−avg_response_time_sec,
future_preference= 1−pattern_now_vs_later_score.
Eachtraitusesasubsetofthesetransformedfeatures. Forexample, asimplebehaviour-based
Conscientiousness score can be defined as
Conscientiousness behaviour =completion_good+sessions_good+skip_good+idle_good+hard_good

7 Five Borrower Scenarios
This section introduces five fictional borrowers and illustrates how their content responses and
metadata patterns translate into trait scores. The numbers are illustrative but consistent with
the scoring logic.
Borrower 1: Sara
Saraisahighlyorganised, future-orientedborrowerwhofinishestasksandengagescarefullywith
the assessment. She chooses future rewards in time preference questions, prefers safe business
projects, returns extra money immediately in the honesty scenario, and selects the cheaper loan
correctly. Her metadata shows full completion in one session, very low skip rates, good focus,
and strong reading of instructions and terms.
Borrower 2: Mamo
Mamo shows impulsive behaviour and present bias. In content, he often chooses “now” rather
than “later”, gives low conscientiousness answers, is strongly drawn to risky options, and chooses
the more expensive loan in the financial decision item. Metadata shows fast responses, many
rapid clicks, multiple sessions, higher skip rates, and more inconsistent answers.

10 Summary and Next Steps
This report demonstrates how FrankScore can:
•Define a clear set of fifteen psychometric traits relevant to creditworthiness.
•Capture rich behavioural metadata (forty-seven features) from the digital assessment pro-
cess.
•Design content (questions and scenarios) that maps explicitly to these traits.
•Combine content-based and behaviour-based evidence to obtain explainable trait scores for
each borrower.
•Represent these outputs as normalised values from zero to one, ready to feed into credit
models.
The next steps for the project are to calibrate the scoring rules using real repayment out-
comes, test fairness and stability across subgroups, and integrate the psychometric trait scores
into the broader FrankScore credit scoring pipeline.
